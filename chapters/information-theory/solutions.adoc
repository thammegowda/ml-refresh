[#sol-ex-information-surprisal.solution]
====
Surprisal is stem:[-\log_2 p] bits, or stem:[-\ln p] nats:

* A fair coin landing heads, stem:[p = 1/2]: 1 bit, or 0.693 nats.
* A fair die showing six, stem:[p = 1/6]: 2.585 bits, or 1.792 nats.
* One token from a uniform 128,000-token vocabulary: stem:[\log_2 128{,}000 \approx 16.97] bits, or
  11.76 nats.

[#sol-ex-information-uniform.solution]
====
With stem:[u(x) = 1/K],

[latexmath]
++++
\KL(p \,\Vert\, u) = \sum_x p(x) \log \big(K p(x)\big) = \log K + \sum_x p(x) \log p(x) = \log K - H(p) .
++++

Gibbs' inequality makes the left side nonnegative, so stem:[H(p) \le \log K], with equality
exactly when stem:[p = u]. The tests check the identity for 200 random distributions.
====

[#sol-ex-information-gibbs.solution]
====
Restrict the sum to outcomes with stem:[p(x) > 0], and let stem:[Z = q(x)/p(x)] with
stem:[x \sim p]. Jensen's inequality for the concave logarithm gives

[latexmath]
++++
-\KL(p \,\Vert\, q) = \E_p\Big[\log \frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\Big] \le \log \E_p\Big[\frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\Big]
= \log \sum_{x:\, p(x) > 0} q(x) \le \log 1 = 0 .
++++

Equality in Jensen's step requires stem:[q/p] to be constant where stem:[p > 0]. Equality in
the last step requires stem:[q] to put all its mass there. Together they force stem:[q = p].
====

[#sol-ex-information-cross-entropy.solution]
====
Group the sum over samples by value. The value stem:[x] appears stem:[N \hat{p}(x)] times, so

[latexmath]
++++
-\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i} \log q_\vtheta(x_i) = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_x N \hat{p}(x) \log q_\vtheta(x) = H(\hat{p}, q_\vtheta) ,
++++

and stem:[H(\hat{p}, q) = H(\hat{p}) + \KL(\hat{p} \Vert q)] splits it into a constant and a
divergence. The tests confirm the equality numerically:

Over *all* distributions, Gibbs' inequality says the minimum is at stem:[q = \hat{p}]: a model
that only memorizes training frequencies, assigning zero probability to anything unseen. Real
models are kept from that point by their parameterization, which cannot represent arbitrary
tables and must share structure across inputs. They are also held back by regularization and
early stopping, and by the softmax, which never outputs an exact zero. The gap between
memorizing stem:[\hat{p}] and generalizing toward stem:[p] is the subject of the next part of the
book.

[#sol-ex-information-gaussian-kl.solution]
====
The log-ratio of the densities is

[latexmath]
++++
\log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} = \log \frac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_1} - \frac{(x - \mu_1)^2}{2\sigma_1^2} + \frac{(x - \mu_2)^2}{2\sigma_2^2} .
++++

Under stem:[p], stem:[\E[(x - \mu_1)^2\] = \sigma_1^2] and
stem:[\E[(x - \mu_2)^2\] = \sigma_1^2 + (\mu_1 - \mu_2)^2]. Taking expectations gives
xref:information-theory.adoc#eq-gaussian-kl[]. With equal parameters the result is
stem:[0 + \tfrac12 - \tfrac12 = 0]. With equal variances it reduces to
stem:[(\mu_1 - \mu_2)^2 / (2\sigma^2)]: quadratic in the distance between the means. The tests
compare the formula with numerical integration for three pairs of Gaussians.
====

[#sol-ex-information-k3.solution]
====
Summing over the support of stem:[q],
stem:[\E_q[r\] = \sum_x q(x) \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} = \sum_x p(x) = 1]. So
stem:[\E_q[r - 1\] = 0] and

[latexmath]
++++
\E_q[k_3] = \E_q[r - 1] + \E_q[-\log r] = 0 + \E_q\Big[\log \frac{q(x)}{p(x)}\Big] = \KL(q \,\Vert\, p) .
++++

For nonnegativity, the logarithm is concave, so it lies below its tangent line at 1:
stem:[\log r \le r - 1] for every stem:[r > 0]. Hence
stem:[k_3 = (r - 1) - \log r \ge 0], with equality only at stem:[r = 1]. Adding the zero-mean term
stem:[r - 1] cancels much of stem:[k_1]'s fluctuation, because stem:[r - 1] and stem:[\log r]
move together.
====

[#sol-ex-information-moment-matching.solution]
====
stem:[\KL(p \Vert q) = -H(p) - \E_p[\log q(x)\]], and only the second term depends on
stem:[q = \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)]:

[latexmath]
++++
-\E_p[\log q(x)] = \log \sigma + \frac{\E_p[(x - \mu)^2]}{2\sigma^2} + \tfrac12 \log 2\pi
= \log \sigma + \frac{\Var_p[x] + (\E_p[x] - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} + \tfrac12 \log 2\pi .
++++

The mean enters only through stem:[(\E_p[x\] - \mu)^2], which is minimized by
stem:[\mu = \E_p[x\]]. Setting the derivative in stem:[\sigma] to zero gives
stem:[\sigma^2 = \Var_p[x\]]. For the two-mode target, the mean is 0 and the variance is
stem:[0.6^2 + 2^2 = 4.36], so stem:[\sigma \approx 2.09]. The grid search in the tests finds
the same values, to within its step size.

The reverse direction is stem:[\E_q[\log q\] - \E_q[\log p\]]. The target's log-density sits
*inside* an expectation over the fitted distribution. For a mixture, that expectation has no
closed form, and the objective has one local minimum per mode. Which one an optimizer finds
depends on where it starts.
====

[#sol-ex-information-mutual.solution]
====
For stem:[\left(\begin{smallmatrix} 0.30 & 0.10 \\ 0.15 & 0.45 \end{smallmatrix}\right)], the
marginals are stem:[p(x) = (0.4, 0.6)] and stem:[p(y) = (0.45, 0.55)]:

* As a KL divergence from the product of the marginals: 0.1258 nats.
* From entropies, stem:[H(X) + H(Y) - H(X, Y)]: the same 0.1258 nats.
* stem:[H(Y) = 0.688] nats and stem:[H(Y \mid X) = H(X, Y) - H(X) = 0.562] nats, so again
  stem:[0.688 - 0.562 = 0.126] nats, or 0.18 bits.

When stem:[Y = X] always, for example with the diagonal table stem:[\diag(0.2, 0.3, 0.5)],
knowing stem:[Y] removes all uncertainty about stem:[X]. Then
stem:[I(X; Y) = H(X) \approx 1.030] nats. For the product of the marginals, the joint equals
the product, and the mutual information is exactly 0. All three cases are checked in the tests.
====
