= Loss Functions & Divergences

A loss is the scalar story a model tells the optimizer. In supervised learning it is usually a
negative log-likelihood: choose a noise model for the target, take minus the log probability of
the observation, and differentiate. This chapter connects that view to regression losses,
binary classification, KL-style distribution matching, and knowledge distillation. Contrastive
losses are left to xref:contrastive-learning.adoc[].

[#sec-losses-nll]
== Losses as negative log-likelihoods

Maximum likelihood chooses parameters that make the observed data likely
(xref:probability.adoc#sec-maximum-likelihood[]). Minimizing a loss is the same procedure after
dropping constants that do not depend on the prediction. If
stem:[y\mid \hat y \sim \mathcal{N}(\hat y,\sigma^2)] with fixed stem:[\sigma], then

[latexmath#eq-gaussian-mse]
++++
-\log p(y\mid \hat y)
=\frac{(y-\hat y)^2}{2\sigma^2}+\log\sigma+\tfrac12\log 2\pi .
++++

The prediction-dependent term is squared error. If the noise is Laplace,
stem:[p(y\mid \hat y)=\frac{1}{2b}\exp(-|y-\hat y|/b)], the loss is absolute error plus a
constant. The probability model is not decoration: it says what kind of residuals the model
expects and how harshly it treats outliers.

For a dataset, the objective is the mean of these per-example negative log-likelihoods. A
constant can be dropped for optimization, but the scale still matters when losses are combined:
doubling a loss doubles its gradient. That is why "MSE" in code must be read with its exact
normalization. Some libraries use stem:[r^2], others use stem:[\tfrac12r^2], and their optima
match but their learning-rate needs differ.

[#sec-regression-losses]
== MSE, MAE, and Huber

Let stem:[r=\hat y-y]. Mean squared error, mean absolute error, and Huber loss are

[latexmath#eq-regression-losses]
++++
\ell_2=r^2,\qquad \ell_1=|r|,\qquad
\ell_\delta =
\begin{cases}\tfrac12 r^2,& |r|\le\delta\\
\delta(|r|-\tfrac12\delta),& |r|>\delta .
\end{cases}
++++

Their gradients with respect to the prediction are stem:[2r], stem:[\sign(r)] away from zero,
and stem:[r] inside the Huber quadratic region but stem:[\delta\sign(r)] outside it
<<huber1964>>. MSE keeps increasing the gradient as an outlier moves farther away, so one bad
example can dominate a small batch. MAE caps every nonzero residual at the same gradient size,
which is robust but has a kink at zero. Huber is the compromise: quadratic near the optimum,
linear in the tails.

This is a robustness statement about gradients, not only about loss values. For residual
stem:[r=10], MSE pushes with gradient 20, while MAE and Huber with stem:[\delta=1] push with
gradient 1. If the large residual is a mislabeled example, the capped gradient protects the
rest of the batch. If it is a genuine rare case, the cap slows learning on exactly the example
you may care about. The loss encodes that trade-off.

[#sec-binary-and-focal]
== Binary cross-entropy and focal loss

For a binary label stem:[y\in\{0,1\}] and logit stem:[z], binary cross-entropy is the negative
log-likelihood of a Bernoulli with probability stem:[\sigma(z)]:

[latexmath#eq-bce]
++++
L=-y\log\sigma(z)-(1-y)\log(1-\sigma(z)).
++++

Using algebra and the same stability idea as softmax, this becomes

[latexmath#eq-bce-stable]
++++
L=\max(z,0)-zy+\log(1+e^{-|z|}), \qquad
\frac{\partial L}{\partial z}=\sigma(z)-y .
++++

The stable form never computes stem:[\log(1-\sigma(z))] after stem:[\sigma(z)] has rounded to
1. Focal loss adds a factor that downweights easy examples <<lin2017focal>>. With
stem:[p_t=\sigma(z)] for stem:[y=1] and stem:[p_t=1-\sigma(z)] for stem:[y=0],

[latexmath#eq-focal]
++++
L_{\mathrm{focal}}=-\alpha_t(1-p_t)^\gamma\log p_t .
++++

When stem:[p_t] is already near 1, the multiplier is tiny; when the example is misclassified,
the loss behaves much more like cross-entropy.

The parameter stem:[\gamma] controls how aggressively easy examples are suppressed; setting
stem:[\gamma=0] recovers weighted BCE. The optional stem:[\alpha_t] balances positive and
negative classes. Focal loss is therefore not a generic "better BCE." It is a targeted fix for
class imbalance where the training signal would otherwise be flooded by many already-correct
examples.

[#sec-kl-losses]
== Divergences as losses

Cross-entropy differs from forward KL by the target entropy, which is constant when the target
distribution is fixed (xref:information-theory.adoc[]). Thus minimizing
stem:[\KL(p\Vert q_\theta)] over model stem:[q_\theta] is the same as fitting the target by
cross-entropy, and its logit gradient is stem:[q_\theta-p]. Reverse KL,
stem:[\KL(q_\theta\Vert p)], averages over the model's own distribution. Its gradient depends
on where the model already puts mass, so it is more mode-seeking and can ignore target modes it
does not sample.

As losses, the two directions answer different questions. Forward KL asks the model to cover
everything the target assigns probability to; putting stem:[q_\theta] near zero where
stem:[p] is positive is expensive. Reverse KL asks whether the model's own samples look
plausible under stem:[p]; it is less bothered by target regions the model never visits. This
distinction is why maximum-likelihood training, distillation, variational inference, and
policy regularization can all say "KL" while behaving differently.

Jensen-Shannon divergence symmetrizes KL by comparing each distribution with their midpoint,
stem:[m=(p+q)/2]:

[latexmath#eq-js]
++++
\operatorname{JS}(p,q)=\tfrac12\KL(p\Vert m)+\tfrac12\KL(q\Vert m).
++++

It is finite and symmetric, but in this book it mostly appears as a diagnostic; training losses
usually use cross-entropy or a directed KL.

The midpoint also prevents the infinite value that ordinary KL gets when one distribution has
support where the other has zero. That makes Jensen-Shannon easier to plot and compare, but its
symmetry removes the useful modelling choice of deciding which distribution supplies the
expectation.

[#sec-distillation]
== Knowledge distillation

Knowledge distillation trains a student to match a teacher distribution rather than only the
hard label <<hinton2015distilling>>. Let
stem:[\vp_T=\softmax(\vz^{\,\mathrm{teacher}}/T)] and
stem:[\vq_T=\softmax(\vz^{\,\mathrm{student}}/T)]. The usual loss is a temperature-scaled
cross-entropy or forward KL:

[latexmath#eq-distillation]
++++
L_{\mathrm{KD}}=T^2\big(-\sum_i p_{T,i}\log q_{T,i}\big).
++++

Without the stem:[T^2], the gradient with respect to the student logits would be
stem:[(\vq_T-\vp_T)/T]. For large stem:[T], both softened distributions move toward uniform and
their difference is stem:[O(1/T)], so the unscaled gradient is stem:[O(1/T^2)]. Multiplying the
loss by stem:[T^2] gives the implemented gradient

[latexmath#eq-distillation-grad]
++++
\nabla_{\vz}L_{\mathrm{KD}}=T(\vq_T-\vp_T),
++++

up to the batch mean. The scale keeps the distillation signal comparable as stem:[T] changes.

Soft targets carry information that a one-hot label discards. If the teacher assigns a little
probability to several similar classes, the student sees that structure in every update. The
temperature makes those dark probabilities visible by flattening the teacher distribution.
The stem:[T^2] factor then prevents the visible signal from shrinking just because the
softening temperature was increased.

[NOTE,caption=In practice]
====
Pretraining and supervised fine-tuning of LLMs are maximum-likelihood training with softmax
cross-entropy. Regression heads choose MSE, MAE, or Huber according to the assumed residual
noise and desired outlier robustness. Focal loss is mainly used when easy negatives overwhelm
rare positives, as in dense detection <<lin2017focal>>. Distillation uses a teacher distribution
and often a temperature, so it can transfer relative preferences among wrong classes, not just
the top label <<hinton2015distilling>>.
====

[#sec-teach]
== Teach it

*The one-sentence version.* A loss is usually a negative log-likelihood; its gradient says how
the prediction should move to make the observed target less surprising.

*An analogy.* Choosing a loss is choosing a judge. MSE is a judge who shouts louder as an error
gets larger; MAE speaks at the same volume for every miss; Huber shouts near the target and
then caps its voice.

*At the board.*

. Write Gaussian NLL and cross out constants to reveal squared error.
. Write Laplace NLL and reveal absolute error.
. Plot MSE, MAE, and Huber gradients against one large residual.
. For distillation, write stem:[(q_T-p_T)/T], then explain why stem:[T^2] is added.

*Misconceptions to address.*

* "Loss names are arbitrary." Most encode a probability model or divergence direction.
* "Robust means ignoring errors." Robust losses still move outliers, but cap their leverage.
* "Forward and reverse KL are interchangeable." Their averaging distributions differ.

*Check for understanding.* Which loss would you choose if 1% of labels are huge measurement
errors, and why?
